## 1. The Problem: Trusted Data From the People Most Affected

When a sudden-onset disaster strikes — a cyclone flattening coastal settlements, a flood isolating inland communities, an earthquake buckling a city — the first people to know what has happened are the people who live there. And for roughly 48 hours, what they know stays local. It does not reach the coordination room.

UNDP's RAPIDA methodology has dramatically shortened the time from crisis to insight by combining satellite imagery, geospatial overlays, and remote analytics. But remote analysis has hard limits. Satellite imagery misses the interior of a collapsed building. It cannot tell you whether the road into a village is cut or passable. It cannot tell you that the community health post is still standing but entirely without supplies. These local-level, granular signals — the ones that allow responders to triage communities and prioritise the first days of action — only exist in the hands and phones of the people who are there.

The challenge is not that affected communities lack relevant information. It is that no reliable, accessible pathway exists to capture that information, structure it for analytical use, and route it to the people who make response decisions. Field enumeration produces rigorous data, but it takes days. Informal social media reports are abundant but unstructured, unverified, and ungeolocated. What is missing is the layer in between: a mechanism that is as accessible as sending a WhatsApp message, as structured as a field survey, and as analytically useful as a GIS-ready dataset.

This proposal describes that layer.

## 2. The Solution: KoboToolbox Community Reporting, Powered by the Kobo–LogIE Integration

The solution is a community-facing damage reporting platform built on KoboToolbox — the humanitarian sector's most widely deployed data collection tool — connected through an enrichment and integration layer to UNDP's downstream systems, including the Logistics Cluster's LogIE platform.

KoboToolbox is not new to UNDP or to the humanitarian ecosystem. It is the standard tool for field data collection across crisis response, used by national and international partners in over 220 countries and territories. The platform that experienced enumerators use to run household surveys is also the platform that community members can use to submit a photo of a damaged building from a smartphone — with the same offline-first architecture, the same geo-tagged media capture, and the same secure, scalable backend.

What is new in this proposal is the application layer on top: a community-optimised form design with accessibility enhancements for no/low literacy users and rapid AI-assisted form localisation, a dedicated integration pipeline that connects KoboToolbox submissions to downstream analytical platforms, AI-assisted enrichment, and a two-sided map interface that gives back aggregated crisis information to the communities who contribute it.

The architecture has three components:

**1. A community-facing reporting interface.** Built on KoboToolbox's web and mobile infrastructure, this is a simplified, high-contrast, icon-assisted form that guides community members through a structured damage submission: one or more photos, infrastructure type, damage classification (minimal / partial / complete), crisis type, debris status, and GPS location or manual building selection on an OpenStreetMap-backed map. The form operates fully offline. Images are compressed in-app before upload. Submissions are queued and synchronised asynchronously when connectivity returns — even on slow networks. Anonymous and pseudonymous reporting is the default. The form interface will support all six core UN languages as a baseline, with a simple mechanism for adding local-language variants.

For community members with low literacy or those who cannot read in the deployment language, the interface is extended by an AI-powered audio capability built into the platform, which converts every form question and hint text into natural-sounding spoken audio, delivering a fully voice-guided submission experience without requiring any reading. This same capability accelerates new-language deployment: entire forms are machine-translated and their audio re-generated in one automated step, allowing UNDP country offices to localise the tool for a new context within hours of a crisis onset.

For community members who cannot obtain a reliable GPS fix — or who can more naturally describe where they are using landmarks — the interface includes an AI-assisted location resolution capability. A user types or speaks a natural-language description ("the collapsed building behind the central market, two blocks from the mosque") and the system converts it to a coordinate with a confidence rating, supporting 100+ languages and humanitarian field vocabulary. Voice input is transcribed automatically, making the location fallback accessible to low-literacy users as well. This directly addresses the scenario where GPS is unavailable, the device location service has failed, or where a precise building footprint match is not possible.

For communities where WhatsApp is already the primary digital communication tool — the majority of crisis-affected geographies in Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Latin America — damage reports can also be submitted directly via WhatsApp, with no app installation required beyond what users already have. The platform's WhatsApp interface converts the same damage assessment form into a conversation. It supports two modes: in **structured mode**, questions are delivered one by one, answers are validated in real time against the form's constraints, and skip logic silently omits irrelevant questions; in **chat mode**, an AI conversational agent conducts a free-form interview, automatically detecting and responding in the user's own language — removing all numbered menus, validation loops, and structured interface friction entirely. Both modes produce a standard submission to KoboToolbox, follow the same enrichment pipeline as any other collection channel, and are indistinguishable from a mobile app submission. Location is captured via WhatsApp's native location-sharing pin; photos and voice notes are transmitted as media attachments.

**2. An enrichment and integration layer.** This is the Kobo–LogIE integration, developed by Kobo in coordination with the WFP LogIE team. Incoming submissions are processed automatically: sensitive metadata (device identifiers, precise coordinates of sensitive infrastructure) is stripped before broader sharing; photos are run through a computer vision model to generate structured severity tags that flag potential classifications for human review or generate detailed descriptions of the damage; voice note descriptions are transcribed, translated and analysed; and administrative P-codes (ADM0–ADM4) are appended via reverse geocoding. For users who have opted into notifications by voluntarily sharing contact details at the end of the form, the pipeline can also route a submission confirmation and PDF summary back to the contributor via email, SMS, or WhatsApp — but this is strictly opt-in and no contact information is collected from anonymous submissions. The enriched payload is forwarded to LogIE, back to KoboToolbox or to another external integration and made available to UNDP analytical systems via API.

**3. A two-sided map dashboard.** (_Note: this is not yet built and would likely (hopefully) be where LogIE takes over._) The community-facing side shows anonymised, aggregated heat maps of reported damage by area — updated in near real-time when connectivity allows — giving contributors visibility on collective impact and a tangible return on their participation. The UNDP/partner-facing side gives coordinators a live, filterable view of geolocated submissions, damage classifications, photo evidence, and trend analysis across infrastructure types and crisis categories. Data can be exported at any time in GeoJSON, CSV, KML, or Shapefile format, ready for direct integration with UNDP's geospatial systems.

## 3. How It Works: From a Community Member's Phone to the Coordination Room

The workflow is designed to require nothing more than a smartphone and five minutes from a community member, while producing structured, analytically useful data for UNDP partners without manual reprocessing.

**Step 1 — Access.** A community member hears about the reporting tool through local broadcast, WhatsApp distribution, email, poster, or word of mouth. They can access it through any of three channels: the KoboCollect mobile app; a browser-based web form; or by sending any message to a designated WhatsApp number — no installation required beyond the app they already have. Each channel opens the same form and produces data through the same pipeline.

**Step 2 — Capture.** The community member photographs the damaged infrastructure, classifies the damage level, selects the infrastructure type, and optionally answers supplementary questions about service disruption (electricity, health, water). In the app or web form, every question is read aloud for low-literacy users; the user taps an icon to replay the prompt before answering. For location, the user confirms a GPS point or selects a building on the map. If GPS is unreliable or unavailable, they type or speak a landmark description ("behind the market, near the collapsed school") which the platform resolves to coordinates — with a confidence rating — without requiring the user to understand or enter GPS data. The entire process works offline before the submission is uploaded. Before the camera opens, the form displays a brief illustrated prompt: stand in front of the building, frame the full facade, no people in the shot. An audio version plays automatically for low-literacy users. A second optional capture step allows close-up photos of specific damage.

Via WhatsApp, the same questions are delivered conversationally — either as a structured one-question-at-a-time sequence or as a free-form chat conversation in the user's own language, guided by an AI agent. The user sends their photo as a WhatsApp image attachment and shares their location using WhatsApp's native location pin. Voice notes are also accepted: they are transcribed, translated if needed, and analysed as part of the enrichment pipeline. Skip logic silently omits questions that don't apply. The AI chat mode handles multi-answer messages, ambiguous phrasing, and mid-conversation language switches — removing all structured interface friction for users who find numbered menus difficult.

**Step 3 — Upload and confirmation.** In the app or web form, the submission uploads immediately if connectivity is available, or queues until it can. The community member receives a confirmation screen with a brief acknowledgement and a live neighbourhood summary — total reports in the area, breakdown by damage level, and the most-reported infrastructure type. Via WhatsApp, the bot sends a confirmation message in the same conversation thread immediately after the form is complete, including the same neighbourhood summary. At the end of the form, users in all channels are optionally invited to share a contact (email, phone, or WhatsApp) to receive ongoing area updates. Enrolled users receive periodic notifications: new damage reports nearby, response teams coordinating in their zone, aid deliveries underway, and status changes. All contact details are held only in the secure backend. Notification frequency is managed to avoid fatigue; users can opt out at any time.

**Step 4 — Enrichment.** The Kobo–LogIE integration layer processes the submission: image analysis generates initial severity flags; voice notes (if submitted) are transcribed and translated (if needed); P-codes are appended; sensitive metadata is stripped. This processing is automatic and takes seconds.

**Step 5 — Coordination use.** Enriched submissions appear as geolocated records in KoboToolbox, LogIE and in UNDP's analytical dashboard, with photo evidence, damage classification, and administrative-level filtering. Field teams can export the dataset for integration with satellite imagery, field assessments, and recovery planning tools.

## 4. What KoboToolbox Brings: Demonstrated Capability at Scale

This proposal is not a prototype built for this challenge. It is an extension of existing, deployed infrastructure with a demonstrated record in humanitarian response.

KoboToolbox is currently used by more than 32,000 organisations and processes millions of submissions per year across crisis contexts worldwide. It is the endorsed data collection platform of UNHCR, WHO, UNICEF, WFP, OCHA, and dozens of national civil protection authorities. The offline-first architecture, scalable submission handling, and open API that this proposal depends on are not future capabilities — they are operational today.

The Kobo–LogIE integration has been deployed across two live humanitarian responses. During the 2026 Mozambique floods, KoboCollect was used for aerial direct observation assessment during UNDAC-led reconnaissance flights: observers submitted geo-tagged photos and structured assessments from aircraft windows, and the integration layer forwarded enriched submissions to LogIE and to MapAction's cartographic products without manual reprocessing. During the Hurricane Melissa response in Jamaica in late 2025, field teams delivering aid submitted damage and access reports on the ground; these fed directly into LogIE and MapAction's static maps, giving response coordinators a live, shared picture of road conditions, damaged infrastructure, and access constraints that guided the routing of aid deliveries and medical supplies. Both deployments validated the core technical flow — KoboToolbox form → enrichment layer → LogIE → coordination products — in active crisis response conditions.

The community reporting application described in this proposal applies the same integration to a different input source: community members on the ground rather than observers in aircraft or aid teams. The data flow is identical. The form design and UX requirements differ substantially — the community interface must be accessible to non-technical users, intuitive under stress, and supportive of anonymous reporting — and this is where the development investment in this proposal is concentrated.

The scalability requirement (up to 500,000 reports per crisis) is well within the operating parameters of KoboToolbox's existing infrastructure, which is designed to handle high-volume, concurrent data collection across multiple active deployments simultaneously.

## 6. Addressing the Core Requirements

**Multiple access channels, including WhatsApp.** The challenge requirements explicitly list WhatsApp as an accepted submission channel. This proposal delivers it as a first-class interface, requiring no installation beyond what users already have. In regions where WhatsApp has near-universal penetration — Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, Latin America — this is the lowest-friction path to first submission. The WhatsApp interface supports both a structured question-by-question mode and a free-form AI chat mode that responds in the user's own language. Both produce standard KoboToolbox submissions that flow through the same enrichment pipeline as app and web submissions.

**Offline and low-bandwidth operation.** KoboToolbox is built offline-first. The community reporting application uses the offline web form and KoboCollect's existing offline queue — data captured with no connectivity uploads automatically when the device re-establishes any network connection, including 2G. Images are compressed in-app before transmission. The WhatsApp channel functions on the same low-bandwidth connections WhatsApp itself already supports, with message queuing handling intermittent connectivity gracefully.

**Multilingual support.** The application will launch with all six core UN languages. KoboToolbox's form language architecture supports additional languages through XLSForm translation columns. The platform's AI-powered audio and translation capability accelerates this further: entire forms — questions, hints, choice labels — can be machine-translated into a new language and audio regenerated in a single automated run, allowing country offices to localise the tool for a new context within hours of activation. Audio delivery also removes the literacy requirement entirely, serving communities where even the six UN languages may not be read fluently.

**Geolocation and building footprints.** The form integrates OpenStreetMap basemaps allowing community members to select a specific building rather than relying solely on GPS coordinates. GPS capture includes accuracy metadata. Manual pin-drop is available as a secondary fallback. Where GPS is unavailable or unreliable, the platform provides a third option: the user types or speaks a landmark description in any language, and an AI pipeline resolves it to a coordinate with a confidence rating and a human-readable warning if the result is approximate. This directly addresses the UNDP requirement for textual location description when GPS access fails, and is optimised for the vague, landmark-relative phrasing that community members naturally use.

**Data privacy and security.** Anonymous and pseudonymous reporting is the default. Device serial numbers and personally identifiable metadata are stripped before data is shared beyond the Kobo backend. GPS precision is configurable — high-precision data is retained in the secure backend, while the community-facing map shows only aggregated outputs. Data is encrypted in transit and at rest. KoboToolbox's data governance framework is aligned with UNDP's Personal Data Protection and Privacy Policy.

**AI-assisted features with human oversight.** Computer vision analysis on submitted photos generates preliminary damage severity flags. These are presented to coordinators as suggested classifications with confidence scores — not as definitive outputs — and require human confirmation before being propagated to downstream products. The AI model's classification logic (structural features associated with each damage tier) is fully documented in the technical submission.

**Modular, extensible form design.** KoboToolbox's XLSForm architecture treats the damage assessment form as a configurable template, not a fixed product. UNDP can add, remove, or swap question modules per deployment — appending livelihood impact questions in the weeks after a crisis, adding health service disruption fields for a specific context, or enabling the supplementary modules described in Appendix 1 of the challenge — without any code changes. Each module is a self-contained group of questions that can be activated or deactivated in the form definition in minutes. This directly supports UNDP's stated interest in solutions that can ask specific, modular questions to specific communities across the lifecycle of a crisis response.

**Redundancy detection and versioning.** The integration layer applies location-based deduplication to identify multiple reports from the same building, surfaces the most recent complete report as the primary record, and logs the submission history for analyst review.

**Non-monetary engagement mechanisms.** Immediately after submitting, contributors see a confirmation screen with an acknowledgement message and a live neighbourhood summary: total reports in the area, damage level breakdown, and the most-reported infrastructure type. This gives contributors immediate visibility on collective impact and a tangible sense of participation.

Users are optionally invited to share an email address, phone number, or WhatsApp contact at the end of the form. Enrolled users receive periodic notifications for their area: new damage reports nearby, response teams coordinating in their zone, aid deliveries scheduled or underway, and status changes as the response progresses. This "information as a service" model — where contributing data earns back relevant, locally useful information — directly incentivises continued participation without monetary reward. All contact details are stored only in the secure backend; notification frequency is managed to avoid fatigue; users can opt out at any time.

## Submission Form Responses

### 4. Problem & Opportunity

The first 48 hours after a sudden-onset crisis are the most critical window for response decision-making — and the most data-poor. UNDP's RAPIDA methodology has transformed early recovery planning by combining satellite analysis with field data, but both streams have a fundamental gap: neither captures the granular, ground-level picture that only affected communities can provide. Ground assessments take days. Satellite imagery is delayed, cloud-affected, and lacks qualitative detail. The people who know what has happened — because they are there — have no structured pathway to share that knowledge with the people making response decisions.

The innovation in this proposal is not the idea of community reporting. It is the combination of the world's most widely used humanitarian data collection infrastructure (KoboToolbox), a purpose-designed community user experience, and an active integration pipeline to UNDP's downstream coordination systems — assembled into a coherent, deployable solution that works in the conditions that matter: offline, low-bandwidth, multilingual, anonymous.

The point of difference is that this solution does not start from scratch. KoboToolbox is already deployed across the humanitarian ecosystem. The integration layer connecting it to the Logistics Cluster's LogIE platform already exists and has been piloted in live response operations. What this proposal adds is the community-facing application layer on top — the user experience, the incentive mechanisms, the two-sided dashboard — that turns existing infrastructure into a community crisis reporting system.

Three integrated capabilities extend the solution's reach to populations that standard form-based approaches miss. An AI-powered audio capability removes the literacy requirement entirely by generating natural-sounding speech for every question and choice label — critical in crisis contexts where affected populations may speak a local language but cannot read even a translated form. An AI-assisted location resolution capability converts natural-language descriptions ("the health post behind the main market") to coordinates, providing a reliable submission pathway for users who cannot obtain a GPS fix or who do not think in terms of coordinates. And a WhatsApp interface lets affected communities submit a damage report without downloading anything — using the app already on their phone. In the regions where this matters most, WhatsApp works on 2G, works offline for media capture, and is already how communities communicate. An AI chat mode goes further: it conducts a free-form interview in any language, handles ambiguous answers, and produces a validated, structured submission — dramatically lowering the cognitive barrier to participation for low-literacy and first-time users.

The result is a solution that is faster to deploy, more credible to institutional partners (because it runs on infrastructure they already trust), accessible to a wider community than any single-channel tool, and more sustainable long-term than a bespoke application built for this challenge alone.

In chat mode, an AI agent conducts a free-form interview in the user's own language — detecting language automatically, handling multi-answer messages and ambiguous phrasing, and extracting a clean structured answer set on completion. Photos are sent as image attachments; location is captured via WhatsApp's native location-sharing pin.

All three channels produce identical outputs: a validated KoboToolbox submission with photo, damage classification, infrastructure type, crisis type, GPS coordinates, and timestamp. Six core UN languages are supported across all channels at launch; AI-powered translation and audio generation enable localisation within hours of a new deployment. Anonymous by default, with optional pseudonymous contact sharing for ongoing area updates. Post-submission, contributors receive an acknowledgement and a live neighbourhood summary — total reports in the area, damage level breakdown, most-reported infrastructure type — giving immediate visibility on collective impact. This "information as a service" loop directly incentivises sustained participation.

The form is built on KoboToolbox's XLSForm architecture, which treats the damage assessment as a configurable template. **Enrichment and integration layer.** Automated processing pipeline connecting KoboToolbox to UNDP's downstream systems. Export in GeoJSON, CSV, KML, and Shapefile. API-first architecture for integration with UNDP's existing geospatial systems. PDF summary reports for high-level decision-makers.

The backend is KoboToolbox's existing cloud infrastructure, designed for concurrent high-volume data collection across multiple active crises. The database schema is documented, open, and structured to support the submission volumes specified in the challenge requirements (up to 500,000 reports per crisis event).

### 8. Solution Risks

**Risk: Low community uptake in the first 48 hours.** The most acute challenge for any community reporting tool is reaching affected populations at the moment they can provide the most useful data. Mitigation: rapid deployment SOPs address distribution channels (SMS broadcast, WhatsApp network activation, coordination partner dissemination), and the offline-first design means the form can be pre-loaded and distributed before connectivity is disrupted.

**Risk: Data quality and reliability.** Community-sourced damage data will be uneven in quality. Mitigation: the form's structured submission schema and in-line guidance maximises consistency; the AI-assisted severity flag and human verification step add a quality layer before data enters downstream products; deduplication and versioning logic prevents early, incomplete reports from permanently skewing the picture.

**Risk: Data privacy in conflict settings.** GPS data and photographic evidence can pose security risks for submitters in conflict zones. Mitigation: anonymous reporting is the default; precise GPS coordinates are restricted to the secure backend; the community-facing map shows only aggregated outputs; image analysis strips EXIF metadata and device identifiers before data is shared beyond the Kobo platform.

**Risk: Deployment speed.** If the integration layer or community interface requires significant configuration time per deployment, the 48-hour window will be missed. Mitigation: the SOPs are designed for rapid, low-configuration deployment by country office staff; language pre-configuration and form publishing are one-step operations in KoboToolbox; the integration pipeline runs automatically once activated.

**Risk: AI misclassification.** Computer vision analysis of damage may produce incorrect severity flags, particularly for damage types or contexts outside the training distribution. Mitigation: AI outputs are explicitly framed as suggestions, presented with confidence intervals, and require human confirmation before use. The model's classification criteria are fully documented.

**Risk: WhatsApp channel dependency on third-party messaging infrastructure.** The WhatsApp collection channel depends on external messaging infrastructure. An outage would interrupt that channel. Mitigation: the app and web form channels remain fully operational independently; WhatsApp is an additional access layer, not a single point of failure. For production deployments, the channel can be reconfigured to route through a dedicated WhatsApp Business number directly via Meta's API.
